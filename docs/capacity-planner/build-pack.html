02Executive summary

The problem

Today nobody can answer "do we have the people to deliver what we are selling?" without a spreadsheet and a lot of asking around. The information exists, but it is spread across five places that do not talk to each other:

  • deals and their likely services hours sit on Opportunities;
  • live work sits on Boost Projects;
  • logged hours sit on Boost Sprint 2026;
  • holidays sit on Holiday Requests in Boost HR;
  • who each IC is, what they can do and how much time they have sits mostly in team leads' heads.

The scan in section 04 shows how much is missing. Only 15% of open opportunities carry a win probability. Only 55% of live projects have a timeline. The Team field on the hours log is unset for most entries. No board records anyone's skills or level.

The answer

We add a Delivery mode to the SDR OS app. It has six new screens, driven by one shared calculation engine:

Overview

For the CEO and leadership. FTE surplus or shortfall by week, by region and by type of work, for the next 4, 8, 12 or 26 weeks. Shows where we need to hire or train.

Team

A heatmap of every IC, week by week, coloured by how full they are. Holidays are shaded. Click any cell to see what makes it up.

Demand

Live projects and weighted pipeline on one timeline. It shows each project's engagement model, on-site needs and whether anyone is assigned yet.

Planner

Team leads assign and rebalance work. The "who's best" ranking scores skill, level, region and free time. When a key person is overbooked, it suggests moves.

People

The IC roster: hours per week, region, monday level (L1 offline to L5 managed services) and a 0 to 3 skill matrix.

My Schedule

Each IC sees their own jobs, start and end dates, planned hours by week and holidays. Each person's My Missing Data list covers the fields that drive the plan for their role.

Sales stays in Sales mode. The opportunity panel they already use gains a Delivery section:

  • engagement model (fixed, flexible drawdown, managed services);
  • services hours and delivery window;
  • products and workstreams;
  • on-site needs, region and complexity;
  • a live "who's free" check.

The data stays on monday boards. The app is the friendly front end, so nobody has to jump between boards.

Fast by design

Everything is pre-calculated before you open the app

A small back-end job inside monday reads every board and does all the maths. It stores the finished numbers at 03:00 and again at 12:00 UK time, with both times set on the Settings board. Opening any Delivery screen then takes under 1.5 seconds, instead of querying boards while you wait. A Refresh button rebuilds the numbers in about a minute whenever you want, for example at lunchtime. Anything you save in the app shows straight away for you, and for everyone else within seconds. The same approach can speed up today's Scoreboard, Pipeline, Revenue and Events tabs. Section 19A has the detail.

The rule that matters most

Probability-weighted demand

Every deal that is not yet won counts towards future demand at its services hours × win probability, spread evenly over its delivery working days. Your example: a 60-hour job starting Tuesday 1 December, 6 weeks long, at 20% to win, contributes 60 × 0.2 = 12 hours. That is 0.4 hours a working day over 30 weekdays: 1.6 h in the week of 30 Nov, 2.0 h in each of the next five weeks and 0.4 h on Monday 11 January. If the deal has no probability, a stage default applies, and the gap is flagged to the rep and their team lead. You can switch between four lenses: Committed, Weighted (the default), Best case and Full pipeline. Section 09 has a working calculator.

Where the committee argued

1 · Do we need an Allocations board at all?

BE
Lead back-end · for

Projects has one row per project with an owner and a contributor. It cannot say "Jake fronts it at 70%, Ilay does 30% offline in weeks 3 to 6". Subitems on Projects are already used for monthly invoicing summaries, and on Opportunities for MEDDPICC criteria. Reusing either would collide with live processes.

PO
Product owner · conditional yes

Sam's condition: nobody clicks between boards. So it is a back-end holding board. Every create, edit and release happens in the app's Planner and in the project or opportunity panel.

SM
Scrum master · sequencing

Phase 1 can derive assignments from Project Owner without the board, so value lands two sprints earlier. The board arrives in Phase 2, and nothing from Phase 1 is thrown away.

2 · Store weekly demand on a board, or calculate it in the app?

BE
Lead back-end

Weekly rows for roughly 400 projects, 120 deals and 26 weeks would be about 13,000 items, rewritten every time a date moves. That means automation storms and stale numbers. The app calculates at read time from source fields instead. The calculation is cheap: about 520 work items × 130 working days.

FE
Lead front-end

Agreed, as long as the engine is pure functions with unit tests and loading stays inside budget. We cache board reads for 5 minutes and recompute instantly when a filter or lens changes.

PO
Product owner

We still want history later ("what did we think we'd need?"). Phase 4 takes a weekly snapshot of the summary numbers per person and per category, not every row.

3 · How do we treat deals with no probability?

PO
Product owner

Sales leaders asked for "a good idea of what percentage chance things have got". Using 0% for a blank hides demand. Using 100% overstates it. We use a stage default and badge it "estimated". The rep sees it in My Missing Data, and the leader sees it on a data quality scorecard.

BE
Lead back-end

Stage defaults live on the Settings board, so leadership can tune them. In Phase 4 they are calibrated against actual win rates by stage.

4 · Skills: separate board, or columns on the roster?

FE
Lead front-end

The ranking and the heatmap filters need every person's skills in one read. Twelve status columns on one roster row is one query and a grid people can edit inline.

BE
Lead back-end

A separate assessments board would give history and evidence, at the cost of a join on every read. Decision: columns now, with every change logged as an update on the roster item through the app. Revisit if HR wants formal assessments.

5 · Who can see what?

PO
Product owner

Sam is happy to share everything for now. So all screens are readable by everyone. Edits are limited by a role on the roster (Admin, Team lead, IC, Sales, Viewer). The real lock is monday's own board and column permissions, which only work if the app writes as the signed-in user (D-15).

SM
Scrum master

That makes the token change the first story of Phase 0. Nothing that writes ships before it.

The current journey, and where it breaks

Today: "Can we start Acme on 1 December?"

  1. The rep asks in Slack, or asks the team lead directly.
  2. The team lead checks Projects filtered by owner, but half the rows have no dates.
  3. Checks holiday messages, then guesses at pipeline.
  4. Asks two ICs whether they have room.
  5. Answers in 1 to 3 days, with low confidence.

Target: the same question

  1. The rep opens the deal and fills the Delivery section (2 minutes).
  2. "Who's free" shows the top 5 matching ICs and their free hours for the window.
  3. The rep requests a resource. The team lead sees it in Planner with context.
  4. The team lead assigns the person tentatively. It turns firm when the deal is Won.
  5. The answer is ready in minutes, with clear confidence.

Fallbacks when dates are missing (always flagged as Estimated)

  • Deal with Date anchor = From close date, or no Delivery start. 9.6 Who carries the demand

  1. Allocations first (Phase 2). Each allocation row gives a person part of the work item's demand. It can be limited to its own date range, for example BA on site in weeks 1 and 2 only. Hours-per-week allocations are taken out of the item's daily demand first, capped at that demand, so they never add hours the item does not have. One that exceeds the item's demand is flagged "Over-allocated". Share % allocations then take their share of what is left. Shares on one item may not total more than 100%. The app blocks that, and a manual edit on the board is flagged.
  2. The remainder goes to owners. Any daily demand not covered by share allocations is split evenly across the item's default carriers:
    • for projects, the Project Owner people who are on the roster;
    • for deals, the proposed IC/CSM people, marked tentative.
  3. Off-roster owners. If an owner is not on the roster (for example a salesperson), their part is reported as "Off-roster". It is not counted against anyone's capacity, and it is flagged for data quality.
  4. No carrier. The demand is unassigned. It counts in team totals and gaps, and it lists the required skills, level and region so the right person can be found.

Phase 1: coverage ratio. For each category and week:

  • Demand is the category's demand for that week.
  • Qualified capacity is the total capacity of the people who meet the category's level and skill rule.
  • Coverage is demand divided by qualified capacity. Above 85% is flagged amber, and above 100% red.

This is easy to explain, but it overstates supply when one person qualifies for several categories.

Phase 3: exact shortfall (max-flow). Each week we solve a small flow network: source → category (demand) → qualified person (∞) → sink (capacity). The maximum flow is the most demand the team could cover if work were reassigned perfectly. A category's shortfall is its demand minus the flow through it. That shortfall cannot be fixed by rebalancing. It needs hiring, training or moving dates, which is exactly the CEO's "train people on MS" signal. With 15 categories × 60 people the graph solves in under 5 ms in the browser.

9.10 Move suggestions (Phase 3)

When it runs. Person X is over capacity in weeks W, or X is needed for a new job J and is short by H hours.

  1. List movable work. Take X's allocations and default-owned items that overlap W.
    • Skip anything marked Locked.
    • Skip on-site work X is the only one qualified to do.
    • Skip the Face role on projects that start or end within move.face_lock_weeks (2). Continuity matters most there.
  2. Find a substitute. For each candidate piece of work, run the ranking for its own requirements, with X excluded. Keep the best substitute Y whose free hours in W cover at least 80% of that work.
  3. Score the disruption. Each move gets a disruption cost by role (move.cost.*): Shadow 0.5, Offline 1, BA 2, Face 3. Add 1 if X logged a client call on that project in the last 7 days (Sprint "Activity Type (IC's)" is Kickoff, Discovery, Sync or Consultation Call).
  4. Rank and choose. Rank moves by hours freed ÷ disruption cost. Pick moves greedily until H is covered, or show the best partial plan.
  5. Apply as tentative. "Apply" creates tentative allocations for Y and ends X's. The team lead confirms them, and nothing changes silently.

What is deliberately not automated

19Front-end and back-end design

How the code is organised, how data flows and how the build stays safe in a repo that auto-deploys to production on every push to main. Design ids: DS-010 is the Sprint read, DS-011 the engine, DS-012 the data layer and DS-013 the Delivery UI. It follows the existing SDR OS conventions: React 18 function components, Tailwind tokens, a single gql() entry point and plain JavaScript modules.

Two things to fix before any Delivery code ships
  1. The token in the bundle. .github/workflows/deploy.yml builds production with VITE_MONDAY_API_TOKEN: ${{ secrets.VITE_MONDAY_API_TOKEN }}. Vite inlines every VITE_ variable into client JavaScript, so a personal API token is downloaded by every app user. gql() uses it for every call whenever it is set. That bypasses the viewer's permissions and stamps the token owner on every write. P-01 removes it, moves to seamless auth and rotates the token (D-15, R-02).
  2. Auto-deploy on main. Any merge to main is live within minutes. Delivery work therefore lands behind a feature flag (19.7) on feature branches, and merges at sprint review only.

Your brief, 26 Oct: "do lots of summary calculation and loading and storing of data so that whenever you load into the app, all the information is there." The answer is a pre-built read model.   1. Scheduled builds. A monday code scheduled job runs every hour. It reads refresh.times from Settings (default 03:00 and 12:00, Europe/London), and builds only when the current UK time matches. The scheduler works in UTC (S-16), so this pattern handles summer time correctly and lets admins change the times without a release. It uses one of the app's five scheduler slots.
  2. The build. The builder reads every source board in parallel with the app's own OAuth token. A server is not limited by the browser bridge. It runs the engine (the same code package the app uses), so results are identical. It writes the results to the app's shared storage as a set of shards. Last, it flips a pointer to the new snapshot, so nobody ever sees a half-written one.
  3. Opening the app. The app reads the pointer and the shards (about seven small storage reads), then draws the screen. A copy is kept in the browser's IndexedDB. A repeat visit draws instantly from that copy, then checks the pointer and quietly swaps in anything newer.
  4. Manual refresh. The header shows "Data as of 12:00 · 3 saves since · Refresh". Refresh calls the back-end, which checks who is asking (signed session token, S-18) and runs the same build. The button shows progress and swaps in the new data when it is ready, in about a minute. Two limits protect the API budget: a cool-down (refresh.cooldown_minutes, 10) and an allowed-roles setting.
  5. Your own saves. When someone saves in the app (a deal's delivery fields, an allocation, a roster change), the save goes to monday as that person (D-15). The app then patches its own copy and recomputes on the spot, so the screen reflects it at once. It also tells the back-end "item X changed". The back-end re-reads that one item from monday, so it never trusts what the browser sent. It updates the shared snapshot and logs the change. Everyone else sees it the next time they open the app, or straight away through a "1 update" chip if they are already in it.
  6. Edits made directly on boards (not in the app) appear at the next scheduled build or manual refresh. The freshness label says so plainly. Phase 2 can add board webhooks (S-14) so the back-end applies those edits within seconds too.

19A.5 The back-end in practice

  • Hosting. The same monday app gains server-side code (Node) deployed with mapps code:push, in the region that matches the account's data region (Q-09). The scheduler is not available in monday's IL hosting region (S-16).
  • Token. An account admin authorises the app once through monday OAuth. The resulting token does not expire until the app is uninstalled (S-18). It is kept in monday code SecureStorage, and is never in the browser.
  • Endpoints.
    • POST /mndy-cronjob/tick: the hourly scheduler entry point.
    • POST /api/refresh: manual refresh. It checks the session token (signed with the client secret), the role from the roster and the cool-down.
    • POST /api/delta: after an app save. It takes {board, itemId} and re-reads that item.
    • GET /api/status: build progress.
  • Limits. One vCPU, 512 MiB of memory and a 300-second request timeout (S-19). A full build is about 15 to 30 reads and an engine run under a second, well inside them. The Storage API allows 12 requests a second per token, and a build writes about 8 keys.
  • Shared engine. src/capacity/engine becomes a small package imported by both the app and the back-end, so there is one set of maths and one set of tests (D-20).
